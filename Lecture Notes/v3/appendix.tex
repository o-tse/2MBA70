
In this section will provide the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:uniqueness_measures}. For this we need to introduce a few concepts as well as a powerful theorem, called the monotone class theorem.

We start with the definition of an algebra.

\begin{definition}[Algebra's of sets]
A collection $\cA$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is called an \emph{algebra} if
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\emptyset \in \cA$,
\item $\Omega \setminus A \in \cA$ for all $A \in \cA$, and
\item $A \cup B \in \cA$ for every $A, B \in \cA$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Observe that, as the name suggests, every \sigalg/ is indeed and algebra. However, in addition to the properties of an algebra, \sigalgs/ where also closed under countable unions and intersections. We will actually take these properties on their own and define any collection of subsets that have these two properties a monotone class.

\begin{definition}[Monotone classes]
A collection $\cM$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is called a \emph{monotone class} if
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\bigcup_{i \in \bbN} A_i \in \cM$ holds for any increasing family of sets $(A_i)_{i \in \bbN}$ in $\cM$, and
\item $\bigcap_{i \in \bbN} A_i \in \cM$ holds for any decreasing family of sets $(A_i)_{i \in \bbN}$ in $\cM$
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

As we already remarked, any \sigalg/ is a monotone class. However, there are monotone classes that are not algebras and vise versa, there are algebras that are not monotone classes. However, suppose we start with an algebra $\cA$ and we want to turn this into a \sigalg/. Then we at least need to ensure it is also a monotone class. Similar to the construction of $\sigma(\cA)$ we can construct the smallest monotone class that contains $\cA$. Moreover, it turns out, maybe not surprisingly, that the resulting collection is \sigalg/. Even better, it is exactly $\sigma(\cA)$. This is the content of the monotone class theorem. 

\begin{theorem}[Monotone class theorem]\label{thm:monotone_class}
Let $\cA$ be an algebra on $\Omega$ and let $\Xi_\cA$ denote the collection of all monotone classes that contain $\cA$. Then 
\begin{enumerate}
\item the collection defined by
\[
	\cM(\cA) = \bigcap_{\cM \in \Xi_\cA} \cM,
\]
is the smallest monotone class containing $\cA$, and moreover
\item $\cM(\cA)$ is actually the smallest \sigalg/ containing $\cA$, i.e. $\cM(\cA) = \sigma(\cA)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\hfill
\begin{enumerate}
\item From the definition it follows that if $\cM(\cA)$ is a monotone class, it is the smallest containing $\cA$.

We will prove that $\cM(\cA)$ contains the union of all increases family of sets. The proof for the intersection of decreasing sets is similar.

Let $(A_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be a family of increasing sets in $\cM(\cA)$ then this sequences is in $\cM$ for all $\cM \in \Sigma_\cA$. Thus $\bigcup_{n \ge 1} A_n \in \cM$ holds for all $\cM \in \Sigma_\cA$ from which we conclude that $\bigcup_{n \ge 1} A_n \in \cM(\cA)$.
\item Since $\sigma(\cA)$ is itself a monotone class part 1. implies that $\cM(\cA) \subset \sigma(\cA)$. It thus suffices to prove the other inclusion $\sigma(\cA) \subset \cM(\cA)$. For this it actually suffices to prove that $\cM(\cA)$ is a \sigalg/.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item Since $\cA \subset \cM(\cA)$ it follows that $\emptyset \in \cM(\cA)$. Moreover, since $\Omega = \Omega \setminus \emptyset \in \cA$ we also have that $\Omega \in \cM(\cA)$.
\item To show closure under complements we consider the following collection of sets
\[
	\overline{\cM}(\cA) := \{A \subset \Omega \, : \, A^c \in \cM(\cA)\}.
\]
If we can show that $\overline{\cM}(\cA)$ is a monotone class then since the algebra $\cA \subset \overline{\cM}(\cA)$ it follows that $\cM(\cA) \subset \overline{\cM}(\cA)$ and thus $\cM(\cA)$ is closed under complements. We will prove closure under union of increasing sets. The proof for intersections of decreasing sets is similar.

Let $(A_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be an increasing family in $\overline{\cM}(\cA)$ and define $B_n = A_n^c$. Then $(B_n)_{n \ge 1}$ is a decreasing family in $\cM(\cA)$ and thus
\[
	\left(\bigcup_{n \ge 1} A_n \right)^c = \bigcap_{n \ge 1} A_n^c = \bigcap_{n \ge 1} B_n \in \cM(\cA),
\]
from which it follows that $\bigcup_{n \ge 1} A_n \in \overline{\cM}(\cA)$.
\item Let $(A_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be a family of disjoint sets in $\cM(\cA)$ and define $B_k := \bigcup_{n = 1}^k A_n$. Then $(B_k)_{k \ge 1}$ is a increasing family of sets in $\cM(\cA)$ and thus
\[
	\bigcup_{n \ge 1} A_n = \bigcup_{k \ge 1} B_k \in \cM(\cA).
\]
\end{enumerate}

  
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

With the monotone class theorem at hand we can prove the uniqueness theorem for measures.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:uniqueness_measures}]
Define the collection
\[
	\cM := \left\{A \in \cF \, : \, \mu_1(A) = \mu_2(A)\right\}.
\]
The goal of the proof is to show that this is a monotone class. If that is true then, since $\cA \subset \cM$, the monotone class theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:monotone_class}) implies that $\sigma(\cA) = \cM(\cA) \subset \cM$ and hence $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ on $\sigma(\cA)$.

To show that $\cM$ is a monotone class let $(A_i)_{i \in \bbN}$ be an increasing sequence in $\cM$. Since by definition, $\mu_1(A_i) = \mu_2(A_i)$ for all $i \in \bbN$, continuity from below (Proposition [REF]) implies that
\[
	\mu_1\left(\bigcup_{i \in \bbN} A_i\right) = \lim_{i \to \infty} \mu_1(A_i) 
	= \lim_{i \to \infty} \mu_2(A_i) = \mu_2\left(\bigcup_{i \in \bbN} A_i\right),
\]
which implies that $\bigcup_{i \in \bbN} A_i \in \cM$.

Similarly, now let $(A_i)_{i \in \bbN}$ be a decreasing sequence. Again, by definition $\mu_1(A_i) = \mu_2(A_i)$ for all $i \in \bbN$ and moreover $\mu_1(A_1) = \mu(A_1) < \infty$ since both measures are finite. Hence continuity from above (Proposition~\ref{prop:continuity_measure}) implies that
\[
	\mu_1\left(\bigcap_{i \in \bbN} A_i\right) = \lim_{i \to \infty} \mu_1(A_i) 
	= \lim_{i \to \infty} \mu_2(A_i) = \mu_2\left(\bigcap_{i \in \bbN} A_i\right).
\]
It then follows that $\bigcap_{i \in \bbN} A_i \in \cM$ which shows that $\cM$ is indeed a monotone class.
\end{proof}
